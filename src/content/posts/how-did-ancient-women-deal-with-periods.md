Half of humanity has always menstruated. Yet for most of recorded history, that fact went largely unrecorded. Menstruation was too intimate, too ordinary, and — in many cultures — too taboo to commit to papyrus, parchment, or stone. What we know about how ancient women managed their periods comes from scattered medical texts, a handful of archaeological clues, and centuries of oral tradition. It is a history pieced together from fragments, and it is more ingenious than you might expect.

## A History Written in Silence

The most striking thing about the history of menstruation is how little was written about it. Medical writers occasionally weighed in — and their views ranged from the thoughtful to the bizarre — but the everyday practicalities of period management were rarely considered worth documenting. Historians who study this subject have noted that the silence itself is meaningful: menstruation was a private matter, managed quietly by women whose inner lives were seldom the concern of the men who wrote most ancient texts.

Since written records referencing menstruation are so limited, oral histories and indirect evidence become essential. Absorbent organic materials — moss, cloth, grass — decay quickly and rarely survive in the archaeological record in ways that clearly signal their specific use. This means our picture of ancient period management is incomplete by nature, and any confident claim deserves scrutiny.

## The Stone Age and Early Solutions

Evidence suggests that prehistoric women managed menstruation with whatever absorbent materials were close at hand. Some accounts of early period management reference pads fashioned from leather and linen-wrapped moss or sand — organic materials that would absorb blood and could be discarded or washed. These solutions left no reliable archaeological trace, so our knowledge of Stone Age practices is largely inferential. What we can say with confidence is that menstruating women existed in every era and almost certainly improvised, as humans always have.

Across parts of sub-Saharan Africa, traditions of using rolled grass or plant fibers as makeshift absorbents have been recorded ethnographically — suggesting that such improvised methods persisted long into the historical period and likely extended much further back into prehistory.

## Ancient Egypt: Papyrus and Reusable Cloth

The oldest direct historical record of period management comes from ancient Egypt. Egyptian women used softened papyrus — the same plant-based material that served as paper — fashioned into something resembling an early tampon that could be inserted to absorb menstrual blood. As National Geographic reported, this is among the few known historical records of period management, in part because the subject has long been considered taboo. It was far from luxurious: papyrus is fibrous and not particularly absorbent by modern standards, but it was available, adaptable, and evidently workable.

Egyptian women also used reusable loincloths, worn beneath clothing to catch blood during the days of heaviest flow. Washing and reusing cloth was almost certainly the norm across all ancient societies — disposable products in any modern sense simply did not exist. The investment was in durable fabric, not single-use items.

## Ancient Greece: Rags, and a Contested Claim

In ancient Greece, the most common practical solution appears to have been homemade rags — strips of cloth, folded and worn against the body, washed and reused cycle after cycle. This is almost certainly where the English phrase "on the rag" has its distant roots, even if tracing the exact linguistic path through history is difficult.

A claim that circulates widely online — that Hippocrates, the father of ancient Greek medicine, described women inserting lint wrapped around a small piece of wood as a tampon-like device — deserves real caution. Historians of ancient medicine who have studied Greek menstruation in depth have pointed out that this claim is often garbled or exaggerated in popular retellings, sometimes based on misreadings of the original texts. The ancient medical writers did discuss menstruation, but what Hippocrates actually wrote versus what has been creatively paraphrased over the centuries remains a live scholarly debate. When reading confident claims about ancient Greek tampons attributed to specific physicians, treat them as plausible but not established.

## Menstrual Blood, Ritual, and Taboo

Ancient attitudes toward menstruation were not simply squeamish — they were also filled with a kind of cultural awe. Menstrual blood occupied an ambiguous position in many ancient belief systems: sometimes understood as polluting, sometimes as powerfully fertile, sometimes as medicinally potent.

The Roman encyclopedist Pliny the Elder, writing in the first century CE, dedicated substantial passages in his Natural History to the supposed properties of menstrual blood. His accounts ranged from the alarming — that menstrual blood could blight crops and cause iron to rust — to the therapeutic, claiming it could cure certain ailments. These were reports of widespread popular belief, not scientific findings by modern standards. But they reveal how profoundly charged menstruation was as a cultural phenomenon across the ancient Mediterranean world. Similarly, some accounts describe menstrual blood being incorporated into ritual practices and beauty preparations, though such evidence tends to be fragmentary and filtered through the perspectives of writers who were not themselves menstruating.

What varied significantly across the ancient world was not the biology but the meaning. Some societies treated menstruating women as ritually unclean, restricting their participation in religious ceremonies or communal life. Others incorporated menstruation into fertility beliefs or healing traditions. The same biological event was freighted with wildly different social meanings depending on where and when a woman lived.

## Why It Still Matters

The history of how ancient women managed their periods is a history of ingenuity under constraint — of solving a real, recurring physical need with whatever happened to be available, often in silence, often without acknowledgment. The scarcity of written records is itself a form of historical data: it tells us what ancient societies considered worth documenting and whose experiences were deemed worth preserving.

Understanding this history matters because menstruation remains, in many parts of the world, a source of stigma and a practical barrier to girls' education and participation in public life. Knowing that this stigma has a history — that it was constructed, varied across cultures, and sometimes contested — is the first step toward questioning it. Women in ancient Egypt softened papyrus. Women in ancient Greece tore strips of linen. Women in sub-Saharan Africa rolled grass mats. They managed, quietly and resourcefully. They always managed.
