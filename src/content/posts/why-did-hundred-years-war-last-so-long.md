It is one of history's great misnomers. The "Hundred Years' War" lasted not a century but a full 116 years — from 1337 to 1453 — and even that span masks the truth: this was not one continuous conflict but a series of wars, punctuated by truces, dynastic marriages, and exhausted pauses. How did a dispute over feudal estates in southwestern France metastasize into a multi-generational struggle involving five generations of kings that reshaped two nations and changed European warfare forever?

## A Throne Without an Heir

When Charles IV of France died in 1328 without a male heir, he triggered a constitutional crisis medieval Europe was poorly equipped to resolve. Two strong claimants emerged. Philip of Valois, Charles's first cousin, argued he had the stronger claim through the male line. Edward III of England contended that his claim through his mother Isabella — Charles IV's sister — entitled him to the French crown. French nobles, applying what became known as Salic law, which excluded inheritance through or by women, chose Philip. He became Philip VI.

Edward accepted the decision — for a time. But the seeds of conflict were already planted. English kings were not merely foreign claimants to an abstract throne; they were also Dukes of Aquitaine, holding real territory in Gascony in southwestern France. This feudal tangle meant an English king owed homage to a French king for land he actually ruled. When Philip VI moved to confiscate Gascony in 1337 — partly as leverage in a dispute over Scotland — Edward III responded by reviving his claim to the French crown itself. The Hundred Years' War had begun.

## England's Early Victories — and Their Limits

The first phase of the war shocked Europe. English armies, wielding the Welsh longbow with devastating effect, shattered French forces in pitched battle not once but twice. At Crécy in 1346 and again at Poitiers in 1356, English tactical discipline outmatched larger French forces. At Poitiers, Edward's eldest son — known to history as the Black Prince — even captured the French king, John II, and brought him back to London as a prisoner.

These victories were extraordinary, but they exposed a deeper problem: England could win battles yet could not simply absorb France. As contemporaries well understood, France was the richest and most populous kingdom in western Europe. Occupying it permanently required more soldiers, more money, and more sustained administrative will than England — a far smaller realm — could provide indefinitely. The Treaty of Brétigny in 1360 secured substantial territorial concessions for England, and Edward III gave up his formal claim to the French crown. It looked, briefly, like a resolution. It was not.

## Why the War Kept Restarting

The fundamental problem was structural: the two kingdoms were too entangled to cleanly separate. English holdings in France created constant friction, and a new French king, Charles V, systematically recovered territory lost at Brétigny through careful diplomacy and military attrition rather than pitched battle — a strategy that proved far more durable than any cavalry charge. By his death in 1380, France had clawed back most of what it had conceded.

Then internal politics on both sides kept reopening the wound. England's Henry V, crowned in 1413, was an ambitious king who revived the claim to the French throne and launched a fresh invasion. His victory at Agincourt in 1415 became legendary — a heavily outnumbered English force routed a French army — and his subsequent campaign was ruthlessly effective. The Treaty of Troyes in 1420 named Henry V heir to the French throne, bypassing the French Dauphin entirely. For a moment, the English crown seemed within reach of ruling both kingdoms.

Then Henry V died in 1422, still in his mid-thirties, before he could consolidate his gains. His heir, Henry VI, was an infant. France was not a country that could simply be transferred in a treaty.

## Joan of Arc and the Turning Tide

Into this renewed stalemate stepped one of history's most improbable figures. Joan of Arc, a young peasant woman from Domrémy who claimed divine guidance, convinced the uncrowned French heir — Charles, the Dauphin — to let her lead troops to the besieged city of Orléans in 1429. The siege was lifted. Charles was crowned at Reims shortly after. The psychological and political momentum shifted in a way that could not be undone.

Joan was captured by Burgundian allies of England in 1430 and burned at the stake in 1431 — but the cause she had galvanized could no longer be reversed. Charles VII, now a legitimate king in the eyes of his people, gradually rebuilt French royal authority and military power. He reformed the army, brought Burgundy back into alliance with France, and drove English forces from province after province over the following decades.

## The Final Reckoning

The end came not with a dramatic siege or a sweeping peace summit, but with a single battle in southwestern France. At Castillon in 1453, French forces — armed with increasingly effective artillery — defeated an English army. John Talbot, one of England's most celebrated commanders, was killed in the fighting. The English never mounted another serious campaign in France. By the close of 1453, England held only Calais of all its French territories.

No formal peace treaty was ever signed to end the war — the fighting simply stopped. English kings continued claiming the French throne as a legal formality for centuries afterward, a fiction only quietly abandoned in 1801.

## Why It Still Matters

The Hundred Years' War did not simply determine who sat on the French throne. It accelerated the formation of two distinct national identities. France emerged with a stronger monarchy and a people who had found shared cause against a foreign occupier. England, stripped of its continental ambitions, turned its attention toward its own islands and, eventually, the sea. The war also transformed warfare itself: the longbow, improved artillery, and the professional standing armies that began replacing feudal levies all developed within this crucible of conflict. And Joan of Arc — executed as a heretic — was rehabilitated, eventually canonized, and became France's patron saint, a permanent symbol of national resistance.

What began as a dynastic quarrel over feudal real estate turned out to be the forge in which two modern nations were shaped. That is why the question of why it lasted so long is really a question about how nations are born.
