## A Universal Experience With a Surprising History

Everyone sleeps. Across every culture, every era, every corner of the human story, sleep has been an inescapable biological necessity. But the *way* people have slept — the timing, the duration, the setting, and even whether they slept in one unbroken block or two — turns out to be far more varied and historically interesting than modern advice suggests. Researchers studying traditional societies and historians investigating pre-industrial records have overturned many assumptions about what "natural" human sleep actually looks like.

## The Primate Paradox: Why Human Sleep Is Oddly Short

Start with a striking biological fact: compared to other primates, humans are surprisingly light sleepers. Most great apes sleep somewhere between nine and ten hours a day, and they typically do so in trees — building fresh nests each night for safety from ground predators. Humans, by contrast, sleep less and do so on the ground. This is unusual enough that researchers have described it as a genuine evolutionary puzzle.

Research published in the *Annual Review of Anthropology* in 2021 characterized human sleep as anomalous among primates in several ways: it is shorter, involves a greater proportion of REM (rapid eye movement) sleep, and takes place in much more socially consolidated settings. David Samson and Charles Nunn, in an earlier study on sleep intensity and cognition, proposed that humans may have evolved to pack more cognitively restorative REM sleep into a shorter overall period — an adaptation that may have supported the expansion of human cognition and social complexity. The trade-off: sleeping on the ground rather than in trees is riskier, but it may have freed up waking hours for learning, socializing, and tool use.

## What Hunter-Gatherers Actually Do

For most of human history, our species lived without electric lights, alarm clocks, or scheduled work shifts. What did sleep look like in that world? A landmark 2015 study published in *Current Biology*, led by Jerome Siegel of UCLA and colleagues, offered a rare empirical window. The researchers tracked sleep patterns in three traditional societies — the Hadza of Tanzania, the San of Namibia, and the Tsimane of Bolivia — using activity monitors over extended periods.

The findings challenged modern assumptions sharply. On average, members of these communities slept under six and a half hours a night — considerably less than the canonical "eight hours" ideal. They typically did not fall asleep immediately after sunset. Instead, they stayed awake for several hours into the evening, fell asleep as temperatures dropped through the night, and woke near or just before sunrise. Temperature, it turned out, appeared to be a key regulator of sleep timing alongside light. Daily napping was not a regular feature of any of these three groups. The researchers also noted seasonal variation: people slept somewhat longer in winter than in summer. Perhaps most strikingly, insomnia as a clinical complaint — lying awake anxiously, unable to sleep — was essentially absent from these communities.

## Segmented Sleep: The Lost Pattern of Pre-Industrial Nights

Move forward to pre-industrial Europe, and another unexpected finding emerges. Historian A. Roger Ekirch spent years combing through diaries, court records, medical texts, and literature from the centuries before widespread artificial lighting — and discovered that a radically different sleep pattern was once entirely normal across much of the Western world.

Pre-industrial Europeans commonly slept in two distinct segments, separated by an hour or more of quiet wakefulness in the middle of the night. Historical sources refer matter-of-factly to "first sleep" and "second sleep" as ordinary, unremarkable experiences. During the waking interval, people might pray, hold quiet conversations, visit neighbors, or simply lie in calm reflection. Medical writers of the era even recommended this middle-of-the-night interlude as an opportune time for study or contemplation.

This segmented pattern, Ekirch argues, was the default human experience across a wide range of cultures and historical periods. It began to fade in the late seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, particularly in urban areas where coffee houses, street lighting, and a new culture of nighttime commerce began pushing social life deeper into the dark hours. By the industrial era, a consolidated single block of sleep had become the norm in much of the West — and the expectation. People who woke naturally in the night and could not return to sleep immediately began, for the first time, to worry. What had once been ordinary became a problem to solve.

## Where Did They Sleep, and With Whom?

The physical setting of ancient sleep was also radically different from the modern bedroom. For most of human history, solitary sleep in a private room was a privilege available only to the wealthy. Ordinary people slept communally — with family members, servants, and sometimes travelers in the same bed or the same room. Warmth in the absence of central heating was a genuine practical concern, and shared body heat was a resource.

Before beds became standard furniture, people slept on rushes, straw, animal skins, or simple mats. In much of the world, sleeping close to or on the ground remained common well into the modern era. The elaborate four-poster beds of medieval and Renaissance Europe were status symbols precisely because they were exceptional. Outdoor sleeping — on rooftops, in courtyards, or on open platforms — was common in hot climates during summer. The rigid, private, indoor bedroom is, historically speaking, a recent institution.

## Dreams, Darkness, and the Meaning of the Night

Ancient cultures rarely treated sleep as merely functional downtime. The boundary between sleeping and waking was loaded with spiritual significance. Dreams were widely understood as messages — from gods, ancestors, or the dead — and were taken seriously as sources of prophecy and guidance across societies from ancient Mesopotamia to Greece, Egypt, and the pre-Columbian Americas. In the ancient Greek world, dedicated dream temples existed where the sick would sleep on sacred ground hoping for a healing vision. This practice, known as incubation, was a formal religious institution, not a fringe belief.

The fear of darkness was also a practical reality before artificial lighting. The night belonged to spirits, dangers, and those operating outside the law. Sleep was a surrender of consciousness in a world where that vulnerability was real, and the rituals surrounding it — prayers before bed, protective amulets, deliberate sleeping arrangements — reflected exactly that.

## Why It Still Matters

The history of human sleep is not merely academic. As insomnia, sleep deprivation, and anxiety about sleep quality have become widespread modern complaints, researchers and clinicians have increasingly turned to historical and cross-cultural evidence for perspective. If hunter-gatherers in traditional societies functioned effectively on under six and a half hours a night, and if pre-industrial Europeans routinely woke for a quiet hour in the middle of the night without distress, then some of our modern sleep anxiety may itself be a cultural artifact — a product of mismatched expectations rather than genuine biological failure.

The evidence suggests that human sleep is flexible, culturally shaped, and perhaps more resilient than we fear. Understanding how ancient humans actually slept — in short stretches, sometimes in segments, on the ground, surrounded by family — offers a wider and more forgiving frame for thinking about what healthy rest can look like.
