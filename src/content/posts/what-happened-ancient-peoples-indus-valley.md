Around four thousand years ago, one of humanity's most remarkable urban experiments began to quietly unravel. No conquering army torched the cities. No volcano buried them overnight. The streets simply emptied, the granaries fell silent, and the great drains that had once been the envy of the ancient world clogged with mud and rubble. What became of the people who built the Indus Valley Civilization — also called the Harappan civilization — is one of archaeology's most enduring mysteries.

## A Civilization That Rewrote Expectations

The Indus Valley Civilization flourished in what is now Pakistan and northwestern India starting roughly five thousand years ago, making it broadly contemporary with ancient Egypt and Mesopotamia. At its height, it covered an area larger than either of those civilizations, stretching across a vast network of river systems including the Indus and its tributaries. Cities such as Harappa and Mohenjo-daro were planned with a sophistication that still startles researchers: straight streets on a grid, sophisticated drainage and sewage systems, standardized brick sizes, and public structures like the famous Great Bath at Mohenjo-daro. The archaeological record suggests a society organized around trade, craft production, and civic order — and one whose ruins show remarkably little evidence of warfare or monumental royal display compared to its contemporaries.

Then, around 1900 BC, the slow unraveling began. By 1700 BC, many of the great Indus cities had been abandoned.

## The First Signs: Cities Under Stress

The archaeological record of the decline is telling. In the ruins, researchers have found evidence of overcrowding: houses built hastily on top of earlier houses, once-orderly streets encroached upon by makeshift construction. Major civic buildings — including structures like the Great Bath that had once anchored community life — appear to have fallen into disuse and neglect. The careful city planning that had defined the civilization's mature phase gave way to something improvised and strained. Long-distance trade networks, which had connected Harappan merchants with distant regions, contracted. The script the Harappans used, still undeciphered today, disappears from the record. Whatever administrative structure had sustained the cities was visibly breaking down over roughly two hundred years.

## Climate's Killing Blow

For decades, scholars proposed various causes for the collapse: invasion, internal revolt, epidemic. Today, the research community increasingly converges on climate change as a central factor — though debate about its precise role continues. A study published in *Communications Earth & Environment* integrated high-resolution paleoclimate records with palaeohydrological data and found that significant river droughts struck the Harappan heartland during the civilization's period of decline. The Indus Valley Civilization had been built on reliable river systems that irrigated fields and supplied dense cities. When those rivers faltered — their flow reduced by a prolonged shift toward aridity — the agricultural foundations supporting large urban populations became precarious. Research covered by *New Scientist* similarly concluded that climate warming and severe droughts dealt a decisive blow to the civilization around four thousand years ago.

An analysis published through the Indian Institute of Technology Gandhinagar notes that while earlier climate studies from the 1970s through 1990s tended to place the onset of aridity before the civilization's mature urban phase, more recent research has pushed that climatic shift to coincide precisely with the collapse. This does not mean climate was the only cause, but the timing is difficult to dismiss.

## Disease in the Final Centuries

Climate and ecological stress rarely travel alone. Research published in *PLOS One* examined skeletal remains from Harappan burial sites and found evidence suggesting that infectious disease became more prevalent in the civilization's late mature phase, roughly 2200 to 1900 BC. This was also a period of shrinking long-distance exchange networks and changing social conditions — precisely the kind of disruption that allows disease to gain a foothold in populations already stressed by food insecurity and displacement. While disease alone is unlikely to have toppled an entire civilization, it may have accelerated a decline already underway, and it gives a human, bodily dimension to what could otherwise seem like an abstract environmental story.

## Where Did the People Actually Go?

This is where the story becomes genuinely fascinating — and where scholars are still actively working. The Harappans did not simply vanish. The archaeological evidence points toward a large-scale dispersal rather than an annihilation. As the major urban centers in the Indus heartland emptied, smaller settlements proliferated to the east and south, in regions that received more reliable rainfall. The population appears to have migrated toward the foothills of the Himalayas and the Indo-Gangetic Plain, carrying aspects of their material culture with them even as the monumental urban tradition faded.

Some researchers argue that the descendants of Harappan people contributed to the later cultures of South Asia, blending over centuries with other populations. Genetic studies in recent decades have added new complexity to this picture, though the precise relationship between ancient Harappans and modern South Asian populations remains a subject of active scientific and sometimes political debate. What seems clear is that the story of the Indus Valley Civilization is not a story of extinction — it is a story of transformation, dispersal, and survival under sustained duress.

## The Undeciphered Script Problem

One reason the collapse remains so mysterious is that we cannot read what the Harappans wrote. Their script, found on thousands of small stamp seals and carved tablets, has resisted all attempts at decipherment. Without written records, historians cannot know how the Harappans understood their own crisis — whether they interpreted drought as divine punishment, mounted organized political responses to food shortages, or simply made individual decisions to move on. The silence of the script is itself a kind of evidence: by the time the cities were empty, no one was stamping seals anymore. An entire civilization's self-understanding has been lost with its writing system.

## Why It Still Matters

The Indus Valley Civilization collapsed, in the most likely reading of current evidence, because a stable climate regime on which a dense urban society depended shifted beneath it. The rivers dried. The harvests failed. The cities emptied over the course of roughly two centuries. That sequence is not ancient history in a comfortable, distant sense — it is a detailed case study in how quickly complex societies can unravel when their environmental foundations erode. Archaeologists and climate scientists increasingly study the Harappan collapse precisely because it offers a well-preserved example of what civilizational stress under prolonged drought actually looks like in the archaeological record. For a world navigating its own climate pressures, that is not a trivial lesson.
