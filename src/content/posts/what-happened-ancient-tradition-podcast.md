## A Podcast Built on an Ancient Question

For as long as human beings have looked at the night sky and wondered, they have also wondered whether someone—or something—put it there. And if so, did that creator speak? Did it leave behind some trace of itself in the oldest stories, rituals, and symbols of our ancestors?

That question sits at the heart of *The Ancient Tradition*, a podcast hosted by Dr. Jack Logan. The show asks something audacious: what if the world's oldest religions are not separate inventions, each arising independently in isolated cultures, but rather echoes of a single primordial tradition—one imparted to humanity at the very beginning?

It is a premise that has animated mystics, philosophers, and scholars for centuries. And for 71 episodes, Dr. Logan combed through ancient cosmologies, sacred texts, and comparative mythology to test it rigorously—until, in 2026, listeners found the feed had gone quiet.

## The Perennial Question in Religious History

The idea that all religions share a common root is sometimes called the *philosophia perennis*—the perennial philosophy. The Renaissance Neoplatonists believed that Plato, Moses, and Zoroaster all drank from the same ancient well. Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz used the Latin phrase in the seventeenth century, and Aldous Huxley popularized the concept for modern readers in his 1945 anthology of the same name.

Comparative religion as an academic discipline, which took firmer shape in the nineteenth century through scholars like Friedrich Max Müller and later Mircea Eliade, approached this territory more cautiously: not necessarily asserting one original revelation, but documenting the striking parallels that appear across cultures that had no known contact with one another. Sacred trees. Cosmic mountains. Descent into the underworld. The number forty. Flood narratives.

These parallels can be explained in several ways—common human psychology, cultural diffusion along ancient trade routes, or coincidence—and historians continue to debate which explanation fits which case. *The Ancient Tradition* enters this conversation on the more theologically committed end: Dr. Logan's thesis is that the similarities point toward something real, a tradition imparted at the dawn of human history.

## What the Episodes Actually Explored

Over its run, the show covered a remarkable breadth of material. Episode one laid out the central thesis: evidence from the oldest religious writings, cosmologies, and sacred symbols pointing toward a unified original tradition. Episode ten took on nothing less than what Dr. Logan called "the greatest mystery in the world"—the appearance of life in the universe—arguing that ancient sources offer a perspective modern science has not rendered obsolete. Episode fifty examined the idea of a "cosmic temple," the widespread ancient belief that the universe itself was constructed as a sacred dwelling place.

Topics documented from across the podcast's run include Zoroastrian accounts of the first human couple, Masha and Mashiana; the cosmology of the Ngaju Dayak people of Borneo, with their supreme deity Mahatala and the figure of Putir; the symbolism of the sacred tree across disparate cultures; ritual descent narratives; and the recurring significance of the number forty in traditions as different as the Hebrew Bible and indigenous religions around the world.

The breadth is the point. If one culture has a sacred tree, it is local folklore. If dozens of cultures, separated by thousands of miles and millennia, share almost identical tree symbolism with almost identical theological meanings, that starts to look like a pattern demanding explanation. Whether the explanation is divine revelation, deep cultural memory, or the universal architecture of the human mind is precisely the debate *The Ancient Tradition* invites.

## The Shape of the Show

*The Ancient Tradition* earned a devoted audience drawn to the intersection of rigorous comparative religion, ancient history, and large theological questions. Listener ratings on both Apple Podcasts and Spotify reflected strong engagement from people hungry for serious treatment of material that mainstream history podcasts rarely touch.

Dr. Logan also maintained a companion feed, *The Ancient Tradition: Audio Writ*, which published recordings of ancient and modern primary texts directly relevant to the main show's arguments. It was a thoughtful supplement: not just telling listeners what ancient sources said, but putting those sources in their ears so they could evaluate the evidence firsthand.

## Why the Podcast Went Silent

Listeners who visited the show's official website in 2026 found this message: "Dr. Logan has paused the podcast to thoughtfully reexamine its conceptual approach and future direction. In the interim, she'll complete final edits on a fun comparative religion thriller slated for a mid-to-late summer release. 'Til then, keep studying."

A mid-run pause of this kind is more common in independent scholarly podcasting than it might appear. Building a show that draws on Zoroastrian theology, Borneo cosmology, ancient Near Eastern temple symbolism, and comparative mythology across seventy-plus episodes requires constant updating of one's own thinking. As any historian will confirm, the deeper you read, the more you realize what you did not know—and sometimes a thesis that felt sturdy in early episodes grows more complicated, more nuanced, or more interesting by episode seventy.

Rather than push forward on momentum alone, Dr. Logan stepped back. The decision to pause rather than simply continue is, in its own way, a methodologically honest one. The ancient traditions she studies were built on careful transmission and honest examination. It seems fitting that a podcast dedicated to recovering them should aspire to the same standard.

## What Comes Next

The pause is not a cancellation. Dr. Logan's website makes clear she is working rather than resting: the comparative religion thriller she is finishing represents a different mode of reaching the same questions—fiction that dramatizes theological and historical research has a long and respectable tradition, from Umberto Eco's *The Name of the Rose* to countless novels that have smuggled serious ideas about religion and history to readers who might never open an academic monograph.

For listeners awaiting the show's return, the *Audio Writ* companion feed remains available as a resource. And the website's parting instruction—"keep studying"—suggests that Dr. Logan regards the audience not as passive consumers but as fellow inquirers.

## Why It Still Matters

The question at the center of *The Ancient Tradition*—whether humanity once shared a common understanding of the divine—will not be settled by any single podcast or book. It has occupied theologians, anthropologists, and philosophers across centuries, and the evidence remains genuinely contested. Scholars who take the parallels seriously, like Eliade, have been criticized for overstating pattern at the expense of local meaning; those who dismiss them risk missing something that genuine comparative study keeps surfacing.

What *The Ancient Tradition* offered, at its best, was a model of serious popular engagement with primary sources—reading Zoroastrian creation accounts and Ngaju Dayak cosmology and biblical narrative side by side and asking, with rigor and curiosity, what they share. In an era when algorithmic content tends toward the superficial and the certain, a show willing to sit with ancient complexity—and willing to pause when that complexity demands re-examination—is worth keeping an eye on.

The tradition, as Dr. Logan would likely remind us, has always been patient.
